\begin{abstract}
Artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM), frequently unregulated or illegal, is spatially
fragmented and spectrally similar to bare soil, rock and turbid water, so segmentation models
routinely miss it and confuse it with background; continental monitoring on free-tier
hardware, meanwhile, calls for compact, low-latency networks. We present \emph{SPEAR-Net}, a
compact (\num{1.06}\,M-parameter, \num{0.60}\,GFLOPs at $256^2$) segmentation network for
mapping mining on Sentinel-2 imagery. It computes four spectral indices---NDVI, MNDWI, NDTI
and BSI---on the fly from the six visible-to-shortwave-infrared bands and fuses them as an
explainable attention prior over the decoder, trained with a recall-weighted, boundary-aware
objective for small, fragmented targets. On the held-out test split of a globally
distributed, expert-verified dataset with an explicit \emph{artisanal-versus-industrial}
label, the two six-band variants reach \num{0.57}--\num{0.59} mIoU, on par with U-Net,
DeepLabV3+ and U-Net++ (\num{0.56}--\num{0.60}) at \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ fewer parameters
and \num{26}--\num{62}$\times$ fewer operations. The more telling result concerns object
size: on small ($<32^2$\,px) components the concatenated prior recovers \num{0.61}, ahead of
every baseline (\num{0.42}--\num{0.51}) and an RGB-only prior (\num{0.44}), which points to
the near-infrared/shortwave-infrared bands---though the single-seed \num{95}\,\% intervals
overlap across models, so we read the gated variant's \num{0.54} cautiously. The gated model
gives the highest artisanal recall (\num{0.75}) but at low precision ($\approx$\num{0.43}), a
screening rather than an enforcement operating point. The efficiency gains fall on CPU
latency (\num{4.7}--\num{12}$\times$ lower) and peak memory (\num{5}--\num{11}$\times$
lower), not GPU throughput. SPEAR-Net is best read as an accurate, frugal screening tool for
large-area artisanal-mining monitoring, with an honest account of the small-site error and
precision--recall trade-off that remain.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Mining reshapes the land surface worldwide, and its most policy-sensitive form---artisanal
and small-scale mining (ASM)---is also the hardest to watch. ASM is often informal or
outright illegal, it degrades rivers and forests, and it resists governance precisely
because each site is small, short-lived and scattered across the landscape
\citep{maus2022global}. Sentinel-2 \citep{drusch2012sentinel2} is well suited
to the problem: it delivers free, frequent, multispectral coverage over large areas, and
semantic segmentation is the natural way to turn that imagery into mining maps. Yet one
failure keeps recurring, across sensors and network designs. Small, fragmented, low-density
mining is under-detected, and what is detected is easily confused with bare soil, rock or
water, so recall stays low and false alarms stay high
\citep{gallwey2020asm}. The sites that slip through are exactly the ASM
sites regulators most want to find.

The stakes are not only cartographic. ASM is the largest single source of anthropogenic
mercury released to the environment \citep{unep2019mercury}---the pollutant the \num{2013}
Minamata Convention on Mercury was written to curb \citep{minamata2013}---and its spread
drives deforestation and river siltation across the tropics. It is at the same time a
livelihood for an estimated \num{40}\,million people, most of them in low-income regions
\citep{igf2018asm}, so blanket suppression is neither feasible nor just. Governments instead
try to \emph{formalize} the sector: to bring artisanal operations under environmental and
regulatory oversight and to separate them from licensed industrial mining. Formalization
needs to know where mining is happening and, above all, at what scale---the very evidence
that timely, large-area maps of artisanal versus industrial mining could supply, and that
current monitoring largely lacks \citep{nursamsi2024review}.

Two practical obstacles sit on top of this scientific difficulty. First, the strongest
public mining annotations are heavy---pixel-wise datasets that reach tens of gigabytes and
hundreds of thousands of tiles, well beyond the free-tier GPUs (single consumer or cloud
cards with modest memory) that most operational and academic groups actually have. Second,
papers rarely state what their model costs to run, even though the numbers that decide
deployability---parameter count, floating-point operations (FLOPs) and latency---are what
determine whether a method can work at continental scale or on the edge.

We treat both obstacles as design constraints rather than nuisances. Our starting point is a
globally distributed, expert-verified mining dataset whose annotation package is small (tens
of megabytes) and whose imagery is fetched on demand from an open archive, so a
representative subset trains comfortably on a free-tier GPU. The dataset also carries
something unusual and useful: a manually checked \emph{scale} attribute---artisanal versus
industrial---which turns the small-scale, illegal-prone class into a trainable target
instead of a near-empty sliver of pixels. Because we fetch the raw bands ourselves, we
recover the near-infrared (NIR) and shortwave-infrared (SWIR) channels and build a
spectrally grounded prior on them; and because the sites span five continents, we can run a
geographic generalization test that the mining literature rarely reports.

Concretely, we make the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Artisanal-versus-industrial mapping on verified labels with a compact model.}
We frame mining segmentation around the policy-relevant scale distinction and show that a
\num{1.06}\,M-parameter, \num{0.60}\,GFLOPs network is on par on test mIoU with
convolutional baselines that are \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ larger and \num{26}--\num{62}$\times$
more costly (Section~\ref{sec:results-main}).
\item \textbf{A spectral prior that helps where scale does not.} On the hardest small
($<32^2$\,px) sites the concatenated six-band prior recovers more components than
\num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ larger baselines do, while an RGB-only variant does not---evidence
that points to the NIR/SWIR bands. We report this with component counts and \num{95}\,\%
confidence intervals, and we are explicit that the gated variant's margin is within
single-seed noise (Sections~\ref{sec:results-area}, \ref{sec:results-ablation}).
\item \textbf{A recall-oriented objective for fragmented targets.} A compound
present-class-Dice, Tversky, focal and boundary/edge loss recovers both mining scales
without collapsing to background and gives the gated model the highest recall on the
policy-critical artisanal class.
\item \textbf{An honest, reproducible evaluation}: an area-stratified diagnosis of where
detection fails, a compute profile that separates the metrics on which a small model does
and does not win (CPU latency and memory vs.\ GPU throughput), and a one-click, free-tier,
credential-free pipeline; all code and configurations are released.
\end{enumerate}

Our results come from single runs, and we frame the claims accordingly: \emph{parity plus
efficiency}, not accuracy superiority. Where two variants differ by less than run-to-run
noise would explain, we say so instead of dressing it up. All code, configurations and the
data-preparation pipeline are released so the tables can be reproduced end to end.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Mining and ASM mapping from satellite imagery.}
Global inventories of mining land use have been built from manual and semi-automatic
interpretation of optical imagery \citep{maus2022global} and, more recently, from
high-resolution satellite mapping \citep{tang2023mining}. Deep learning is now applied
routinely to mining footprints: Saputra et al.\ \cite{saputra2025multimodal} segmented
mining land covers across 37 sites worldwide from multi-modal imagery, and Balaniuk et al.\
\cite{balaniuk2020mining} detected mining and tailings dams at national scale. Multi-class
systems such as MineCam pair segmentation with change detection over annotated mine
components \citep{jablonska2024minecam}. Work on ASM specifically is closer to ours: Gallwey
et al.\ \cite{gallwey2020asm} trained a multispectral network for small-scale mining in
Ghana, Ngom et al.\ \cite{ngom2020senegal} mapped artisanal gold mining in Senegal from
Sentinel-2, Couttenier et al.\ \cite{couttenier2022asm} covered 1.75 million~km$^2$ of West
Africa with a segmentation network, and Fonseca et al.\ \cite{fonseca2024asm} sharpened ASM
detection using spectral--textural features from Landsat time series. A recent systematic
review \citep{nursamsi2024review} reaches a sobering conclusion: methods that work locally
seldom transfer to regional or global monitoring. Two threads run through all of this work.
Fragmented, low-density mining is hard to recover and easily mistaken for spectrally similar
background---which motivates physical priors and recall-oriented training---and almost none
of these studies reports what the model costs to run, a gap we make central here.

\paragraph{Semantic segmentation architectures.}
Since fully convolutional networks established dense prediction \citep{long2015fcn},
encoder--decoder networks with skip connections, epitomized by U-Net
\citep{ronneberger2015unet} and its successors U-Net++ \citep{zhou2019unetpp} and
Attention U-Net \citep{oktay2018attentionunet}, have remained strong baselines, as have
context-aggregation designs such as PSPNet \citep{zhao2017pspnet}, atrous
encoder--decoders such as DeepLabV3+ \citep{chen2018deeplabv3plus} and transformer
segmenters such as SegFormer \citep{xie2021segformer}. These models are accurate but
parameter-heavy (tens of millions of weights). Efficiency-oriented backbones---MobileNetV2/V3
\citep{sandler2018mobilenetv2}, \citep{howard2019mobilenetv3}, EfficientNet
\citep{tan2019efficientnet} and depthwise-separable convolutions
\citep{chollet2017xception}---enable compact segmenters, but their accuracy--efficiency
trade-off for fine-grained mining segmentation has not been systematically reported.

\paragraph{Spectral indices and prior-guided attention.}
Normalized-difference indices such as NDVI \citep{rouse1974ndvi} and MNDWI
\citep{xu2006mndwi}, and soil/turbidity indices such as BSI \citep{rikimaru2002bsi}, encode
domain knowledge about vegetation, water and bare ground. In mining remote sensing they are
typically used as hand-crafted features for classical classifiers, or fed as raw extra
channels, rather than as a \emph{learned}, spatially explicit attention prior inside a
modern segmentation network. We revisit these indices as a differentiable, explainable gate.

\paragraph{Imbalanced-segmentation losses.}
Region-overlap losses (Dice \citep{milletari2016vnet}, Tversky
\citep{salehi2017tversky}), the focal loss \citep{lin2020focal} and boundary-aware losses
\citep{kervadec2021boundary} were introduced to counter class imbalance and to sharpen
object boundaries. We combine them into a single recall-oriented objective tailored to the
small, fragmented artisanal class.

\paragraph{Geospatial foundation models.}
A parallel line of work pre-trains large transformer backbones on massive unlabelled
satellite archives---masked-autoencoder models such as SatMAE \citep{cong2022satmae},
geospatial foundation models (e.g.\ Prithvi, Clay), and the promptable Segment Anything Model
\citep{kirillov2023sam} adapted to Earth observation---and fine-tunes them for downstream
tasks. These models can be
powerful when abundant compute and labels are available, but they carry hundreds of millions
of parameters and are ill-suited to the free-tier and CPU-only budgets that constrain much
operational and academic ASM monitoring. Our aim is complementary: a compact, transparent
model whose inductive bias comes from explicit spectral indices rather than from large-scale
pre-training, so that it trains and runs within commodity resources while remaining
interpretable. Distilling or adapting a foundation model under the same budget is an
orthogonal and promising direction.

\section{Materials and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Dataset}\label{sec:data}
Our data come from a globally distributed, machine-learning-ready mining dataset
\citep{jasansky2024dataset}, which reconciles and manually re-validates polygons from Maus
et al.\ \cite{maus2022global} and Tang et al.\ \cite{tang2023mining}. For each site the
compact annotation package gives the exact Sentinel-2 scene reference, the mask polygons, a
train/validation/test split, and two labels: a mine type (surface, placer, underground,
brine/evaporation) and---the one we care about---a \emph{scale} label, artisanal or
industrial. In total the annotation layer holds \num{1514} tiles from \num{1128} distinct
Sentinel-2 scenes; \num{1207} of them carry a split (\num{890}/\num{201}/\num{116}), and
those are the tiles we use. Across all tiles the scale split is \num{329} artisanal and
\num{1185} industrial, and across the split subset \num{232}/\num{975} (Table~\ref{tab:data}).
Annotation quality on the test set is high---precision above \num{0.99}, recall above
\num{0.95}---so the scale attribute is reliable enough to train on. Figure~\ref{fig:map}
maps the sites: both scale classes reach across South America, Africa, Asia and Oceania,
which is what makes the held-out continent protocol of Section~\ref{sec:results-gen}
possible.

\subsection{Imagery retrieval and chipping}\label{sec:prep}
We pull Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface-reflectance imagery on demand from an open cloud archive
\citep{planetarycomputer}. For each tile we find the matching scene from its grid tile and
acquisition date and load six bands---blue (B2), green (B3), red (B4), near-infrared (B8),
and shortwave-infrared SWIR1 (B11) and SWIR2 (B12)---on a common \SI{10}{\meter} grid over a
$2048\times2048$ window centred on the site. The mask polygons are rasterized onto the same
grid and coordinate reference system, and each window is cut into non-overlapping
$256\times256$ chips. We keep every mining chip and retain mining-free chips as negatives
with probability \num{0.3} (\num{9034} positive and \num{6237} negative chips). To keep the
pipeline light we take \emph{all} artisanal tiles but cap industrial tiles at \num{80} per
split, for \num{15271} chips in all, and during training we oversample artisanal chips
threefold. The SWIR bands (B11, B12) are natively \num{20}\,m, so we bilinearly resample
them to the \num{10}\,m grid to put all six on one raster---worth flagging, since three of
the four indices use SWIR. Digital numbers are divided by \num{10000} to approximate surface
reflectance $\rho\in[0,1]$. Apart from the L2A product's own atmospheric correction we apply
\emph{no} scene-classification cloud or shadow masking, and each site is a single
acquisition. Chips inherit their tile's split, so no site appears in more than one split and
there is no spatial leakage. Nothing else is preprocessed; the spectral indices below are
computed on the fly inside the data loader.

\subsection{Overview of SPEAR-Net}\label{sec:arch}
SPEAR-Net is an encoder--decoder segmenter with three additions targeted at fine-grained
mining: (i) an ImageNet-pretrained lightweight encoder whose stem is adapted from three to
six input bands; (ii) a spectral-index prior (PISP) that modulates the
decoder skip connections; and (iii) a segmentation head paired with an auxiliary
edge/boundary head for deep supervision. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the architecture and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:spearnet} summarizes a full training step.

\paragraph{Encoder.}
We use MobileNetV3-Small \citep{howard2019mobilenetv3} pretrained on ImageNet
\citep{deng2009imagenet} as a feature extractor, returning multi-scale features at strides
$2$ to $32$. To accept six bands, the first convolution is expanded from three to six input
channels, initializing the additional NIR/SWIR filters by tiling and rescaling the
pretrained RGB filters so that pretrained low-level features are preserved. The decoder is a
U-Net-style path built from depthwise-separable convolution blocks
\citep{chollet2017xception} with channel widths $\{128,64,48,32\}$, progressively upsampling
and fusing gated skip features.

\subsection{Spectral-index prior (PISP)}\label{sec:pisp}
We term this a \emph{spectral-index prior} (PISP) because its channels are normalized band ratios
grounded in the physical reflectance behaviour of surface materials---chlorophyll
absorption and near-infrared scattering for vegetation, strong SWIR absorption by water,
and the high visible/SWIR reflectance of exposed soil---rather than being learned from data; each is a standard remote-sensing proxy, cited where
it is defined below. From the reflectance bands
$\rho_{\mathrm{B}},\rho_{\mathrm{G}},\rho_{\mathrm{R}},\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}},
\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}},\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR2}}$ we compute four indices that encode the
dominant visual cues of mining---loss of vegetation, presence of ponds and turbid water,
and exposed bare ground/tailings:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{NDVI}  &= \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}-\rho_{\mathrm{R}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}+\rho_{\mathrm{R}}}, \label{eq:ndvi}\\[2pt]
\mathrm{MNDWI} &= \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{G}}-\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{G}}+\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}}}, \label{eq:mndwi}\\[2pt]
\mathrm{NDTI}  &= \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{R}}-\rho_{\mathrm{G}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{R}}+\rho_{\mathrm{G}}}, \label{eq:ndti}\\[2pt]
\mathrm{BSI}   &= \frac{(\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}}+\rho_{\mathrm{R}})-(\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}+\rho_{\mathrm{B}})}
                       {(\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}}+\rho_{\mathrm{R}})+(\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}+\rho_{\mathrm{B}})}. \label{eq:bsi}
\end{align}
In practice a small constant $\epsilon=10^{-6}$ is added to each denominator in
Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ndvi}--\eqref{eq:bsi} to guard against division by zero. Stacking the four
indices gives a tensor
$\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times H\times W}$ (NDVI is vegetation
\citep{rouse1974ndvi}, MNDWI water \citep{xu2006mndwi}, NDTI turbidity
\citep{lacaux2007ndti}, BSI bare soil \citep{rikimaru2002bsi}). A small convolutional gate maps $\mathbf{P}$ to a single-channel
spatial attention map,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} = \sigma\!\big(\, g(\mathbf{P}) \,\big),\qquad
g(\mathbf{P}) = \mathrm{Conv}_{1\times1}\!\big(\phi\big(\mathrm{DWConv}_{3\times3}(\phi(\mathrm{Conv}_{1\times1}(\mathbf{P})))\big)\big),
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is a batch-normalized ReLU, $\mathrm{DWConv}$ a depthwise convolution and
$\sigma$ the logistic function, so $\mathbf{A}\in(0,1)^{1\times H\times W}$. The map is
applied multiplicatively to each decoder skip feature $\mathbf{F}_s$ at scale $s$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}'_s = \mathbf{F}_s \odot \big(1 + \alpha_s\, \mathbf{A}_{\downarrow s}\big),
\label{eq:apply}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}_{\downarrow s}$ is $\mathbf{A}$ bilinearly resized to the resolution of
scale $s$, $\alpha_s$ is a learnable per-scale scalar, and $\odot$ is broadcast elementwise
multiplication. The residual form $1+\alpha_s\mathbf{A}$ lets the network fall back to the
ungated feature when the prior is uninformative. Because $\mathbf{A}$ is a single explicit
map derived from named indices, it is directly interpretable as ``where the spectral
evidence for mining is strongest''. As an ablation we also consider a simpler
\emph{concatenation} variant, in which $\mathbf{P}$ is appended to the input bands and
linearly projected back to six channels, and an \emph{RGB-only} variant in which the same
gate is driven by three colour indices computed from the visible bands alone.

\subsection{Recall-weighted, boundary-aware objective}\label{sec:loss}
Let $p_{c}$ and $g_{c}$ denote the predicted probability and one-hot ground truth for class
$c$, and let $\mathcal{C}^{+}$ be the set of classes present in a batch. We combine four
terms. A generalized Dice term \citep{sudre2017generaliseddice},
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}} = 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}^{+}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}^{+}}
\frac{2\sum_i p_{c,i} g_{c,i} + \epsilon}{\sum_i p_{c,i} + \sum_i g_{c,i} + \epsilon},
\label{eq:dice}
\end{equation}
averaged \emph{only over present classes} to avoid a spurious penalty that would otherwise
drive the network toward the majority (background) class. A Tversky term
\citep{salehi2017tversky} with $\alpha<\beta$ penalizes false negatives more than false
positives, directly favouring recall of the rare classes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tv}} = 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}^{+}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}^{+}}
\frac{\sum_i p_{c,i} g_{c,i}}
     {\sum_i p_{c,i} g_{c,i} + \alpha\sum_i p_{c,i}(1-g_{c,i}) + \beta\sum_i (1-p_{c,i}) g_{c,i}}.
\label{eq:tversky}
\end{equation}
A class-weighted focal term \citep{lin2020focal} supplies a per-pixel gradient that focuses
on hard, rare-class pixels,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} w_{y_i}\, (1-p_{y_i,i})^{\gamma}\, \log p_{y_i,i},
\label{eq:focal}
\end{equation}
with $w$ the inverse-frequency class weights and $\gamma$ the focusing parameter. Finally, a
boundary term concentrates a cross-entropy penalty on the ground-truth object boundaries
$\mathbf{B}$ (the Sobel edges of the target mask), and an auxiliary head predicts those
boundaries directly:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{\sum_i \mathbf{B}_i\, \mathrm{CE}_i}{\sum_i \mathbf{B}_i + \epsilon},
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{E}} = \mathrm{BCE}\big(\sigma(\hat{\mathbf{e}}),\, \mathbf{B}\big).
\label{eq:boundary}
\end{equation}
The total objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\mathrm{D}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}}
            + \lambda_{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tv}}
            + \lambda_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}}
            + \lambda_{\mathrm{B}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}
            + \lambda_{\mathrm{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{E}}.
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{SPEAR-Net training step for one mini-batch}
\label{alg:spearnet}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require six-band patch $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times H\times W}$, ground-truth mask
$\mathbf{Y}$, parameters $\theta$, per-scale gate weights $\{\alpha_k\}$
\Statex \textbf{Spectral-index prior}
\State $\mathbf{P}\gets[\mathrm{NDVI},\mathrm{MNDWI},\mathrm{NDTI},\mathrm{BSI}]$ from $\mathbf{X}$ \Comment{Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ndvi}--\eqref{eq:bsi}}
\State $\mathbf{A}\gets\sigma\!\big(g(\mathbf{P})\big)$ \Comment{single-channel attention map, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate}}
\Statex \textbf{Encoder (MobileNetV3-Small, strides $2$--$32$)}
\State $(\mathbf{s}_1,\dots,\mathbf{s}_5)\gets\mathrm{Encoder}(\mathbf{X})$
\Statex \textbf{Prior-gated depthwise-separable decoder}
\State $\mathbf{u}\gets\mathbf{s}_5$ \Comment{ungated bottleneck}
\For{$k=4$ \textbf{down to} $1$}
  \State $\mathbf{u}\gets\mathrm{Upsample}(\mathbf{u})$ to the resolution of $\mathbf{s}_k$
  \State $\mathbf{F}'_k\gets\mathbf{s}_k\odot\big(1+\alpha_k\,\mathbf{A}_{\downarrow k}\big)$ \Comment{gate skip, Eq.~\eqref{eq:apply}}
  \State $\mathbf{u}\gets\mathrm{DSConv}\big([\,\mathbf{u};\,\mathbf{F}'_k\,]\big)$ \Comment{concatenate, then refine}
\EndFor
\State $\mathbf{u}\gets\mathrm{Upsample}_{256^2}(\mathbf{u})$
\Statex \textbf{Heads, objective, and update}
\State $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}\gets\mathrm{SegHead}(\mathbf{u})$;\quad $\hat{\mathbf{e}}\gets\mathrm{EdgeHead}(\mathbf{u})$
\State $\mathbf{B}\gets\mathrm{Sobel}(\mathbf{Y})$ \Comment{boundary target}
\State $\mathcal{L}\gets\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tv}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{B}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{E}}$ \Comment{Eqs.~\eqref{eq:dice}--\eqref{eq:total}}
\State $\theta\gets\mathrm{AdamW}\big(\theta,\ \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}\big)$
\State \Return updated $\theta$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Training details}\label{sec:training}
Unless stated otherwise we use
$\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{T}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}=1$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{B}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{E}}=0.5$, Tversky $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.3,0.7)$, and
focal $\gamma=2$. Class weights $w$ are estimated by median-frequency balancing on a random
sample of masks, and chips containing the rare (artisanal) class are oversampled. Models are
trained with AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} at a base learning rate of
$3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, a cosine schedule \citep{loshchilov2017sgdr} with a
three-epoch linear warm-up, mixed-precision, gradient clipping at norm $1$, batch size
$10$, and $40$ epochs. Implementation uses PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch}; convolutional
baselines use the implementations of \cite{iakubovskii2019smp} with ResNet-34 encoders
\citep{he2016resnet} and the same six-band input, loss and schedule for a controlled
comparison. Training used a single NVIDIA A100
GPU; the model also fits and trains within the memory of a free-tier 16\,GB accelerator.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
With $\mathrm{TP}_c$, $\mathrm{FP}_c$ and $\mathrm{FN}_c$ the pixel-level true positives,
false positives and false negatives of class $c$ accumulated over the evaluation set, we
report the per-class Intersection-over-Union and recall,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IoU}_c \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{TP}_c}{\mathrm{TP}_c+\mathrm{FP}_c+\mathrm{FN}_c},
\qquad
R_c \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{TP}_c}{\mathrm{TP}_c+\mathrm{FN}_c},
\label{eq:iou}
\end{equation}
and their means over the $C$ classes present in the ground truth,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{mIoU} \;=\; \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{IoU}_c,
\qquad
\overline{R} \;=\; \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} R_c .
\label{eq:miou}
\end{equation}
To probe the field-wide small-object failure directly, we additionally compute an
\emph{area-stratified recall} at the object level. Let
$\mathcal{K}_b=\{K_1,\dots,K_{n_b}\}$ be the connected components of the foreground ground
truth whose pixel area falls in bin $b\in\{$small $<32^2$, medium, large $\geq96^2\}$; a
component counts as recovered if at least one of its pixels is predicted with the correct
class, giving
\begin{equation}
R^{\mathrm{area}}_b \;=\; \frac{1}{n_b}\,
\bigl|\{\,K\in\mathcal{K}_b : \hat{Y}\cap K \neq \varnothing \,\}\bigr| ,
\label{eq:areastrat}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{Y}$ denotes the set of pixels predicted as the component's class. This metric
is deliberately lenient at the pixel level so that it isolates \emph{detection} of a site
from delineation quality. Efficiency is reported as parameter count and
multiply--accumulate operations (GFLOPs) at $256^2$ input. For geographic generalization
we assign each chip to a continent from its centroid and perform a held-out continent
experiment, training with two continents held out and testing on them.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
Every number below is on the held-out \emph{test} split; validation served only to pick
checkpoints. All models share the same six-band input, compound loss and schedule, and each
was trained once for \num{40} epochs.

\subsection{Accuracy and the artisanal--industrial distinction}\label{sec:results-main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} gives test-split accuracy. On mIoU the compact SPEAR-Net variants
(\num{0.572}--\num{0.590}) and the RGB variant (\num{0.620}) sit interleaved with the
\num{21}--\num{26}\,M-parameter baselines (\num{0.562}--\num{0.604}); no consistent gap
separates the two groups, so an order-of-magnitude cut in size buys no loss of accuracy
here. Foreground mIoU, with background excluded, tells the same story (\num{0.41}--\num{0.47}
for every model). On the artisanal class---the one that matters for policy---the gated model
has the best recall of any model (\num{0.748} against \num{0.647}--\num{0.662} for the
baselines), which is exactly what its recall-oriented design is for. The baselines keep a
slight edge on the industrial class, unsurprising given how heavily it dominates the test
split (Table~\ref{tab:data}). Figure~\ref{fig:efficiency} plots accuracy against parameter
count.

The pixel-level confusion matrix of the gated model (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}) makes the
error structure explicit. Recall on the diagonal is \num{0.926}/\num{0.748}/\num{0.662} for
background/artisanal/industrial. The dominant artisanal error is omission: \num{24.2}\,\% of
artisanal pixels are assigned to background, whereas only \num{1.0}\,\% are confused with
industrial. The scale confusion is therefore small---an artisanal site is rarely mislabelled
as regulated industrial mining---but the background-omission rate is the operational
bottleneck and is consistent with the small-site recall deficit analysed in
Section~\ref{sec:results-area}. Precision cuts the other way, and we state it plainly:
because the recall-weighted objective deliberately over-predicts the mining classes, the
gated model's per-class precision is \num{0.966} (background), \num{0.431} (artisanal) and
\num{0.571} (industrial), with F1 of \num{0.945}, \num{0.546} and \num{0.613}. At the pixel
level roughly \num{57}\,\% of everything flagged as artisanal is a false alarm---an expected
price of favouring recall, and one we weigh when we discuss the operating point
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Both artisanal off-diagonals are, from an enforcement
standpoint, false negatives (a missed illegal-prone site); that, together with the low
precision, is why we treat the output as a screening aid and report recall and precision
together rather than accuracy.

\subsection{Where detection fails, and what fixes it}\label{sec:results-area}
What decides usefulness in the field is not overall mIoU but whether the small, fragmented
sites get found at all. Table~\ref{tab:area} breaks recall down by ground-truth component
size (Eq.~\ref{eq:areastrat}) for every model, over $n=\num{82}/\num{59}/\num{37}$
small/medium/large components; because these counts are modest, we give both the fraction and
the raw count. Two things stand out. First, recall climbs steeply with size for every
model---from about \num{0.4} on small ($<32^2$\,px) objects to \num{0.8}--\num{0.9} on large
ones---so small sites are the shared bottleneck, and the \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ larger
baselines do not close it (small-object recall \num{0.42}--\num{0.51}, i.e.\ \num{34}--\num{42}
of \num{82} components). Second, the six-band prior helps here: the concatenated prior
recovers \num{50}/\num{82} small components (\num{0.610}) and the gated prior \num{44}/\num{82}
(\num{0.537}), against the best baseline's \num{42}/\num{82} (\num{0.512}), while the RGB-only
prior recovers only \num{36}/\num{82} (\num{0.439})---placing the effect on the NIR/SWIR
bands. These margins must be read with the sample size in mind. The concatenated prior's
lead---eight components over the best baseline---is reasonably robust, but the gated prior's
two-component lead is not, and the \num{95}\,\% Wilson intervals in Figure~\ref{fig:areabars}
overlap across the whole group; we therefore treat the concatenated result as solid and the
gated result as suggestive, pending multi-seed confirmation. Even so, a \num{1.06}\,M-parameter
spectral model recovering more small sites than networks with \num{20}--\num{25}$\times$ its
parameters is the operationally relevant point. And we read the absolute value honestly: at
\num{0.44}--\num{0.61} small-object recall this is a \emph{screening and prioritisation} aid,
not enforcement-grade detection.

\subsection{Component analysis (ablation)}\label{sec:results-ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} turns each component on in turn. Every row uses the same chips,
splits, random seed and schedule, and the RGB variant runs on the same six-band chips
restricted to the visible bands, so the six-band-versus-RGB comparison is clean. Adding the
recall-weighted loss to the plain backbone lifts overall recall (\num{0.573}/\num{0.586}
$\rightarrow$ \num{0.605}/\num{0.627} on the two mining classes) and small-object recall
(\num{0.366}$\rightarrow$\num{0.463}). The six-band prior pushes small-object recall higher
still, most sharply in the concatenated form (\num{0.610}); the gated form gives a little of
that back in exchange for the highest artisanal recall (\num{0.748}). The RGB variant goes
the other way and \emph{loses} small-object recall (\num{0.439}), which confirms the gain
rides on the NIR/SWIR bands rather than on the gating mechanism itself. That same RGB variant
nonetheless posts the highest overall mIoU (\num{0.620}) and foreground mIoU (\num{0.471}) in
the study, and we address that head-on rather than bury it. We read it as a composition
effect, not as colour beating spectra: the test split is industrial-heavy
(Table~\ref{tab:data}) and industrial sites are large and contiguous, so a variant that
trades small-site recall for cleaner background and large-object delineation is rewarded by an
area-weighted aggregate like mIoU. The six-band priors make the opposite trade---more small
sites at slightly lower mIoU---which is exactly why we treat small-object recall
(Table~\ref{tab:area}), not mIoU, as the operationally decisive measure. The comparison is
controlled: the RGB variant runs on the same six-band chips restricted to the visible bands,
under the same splits, seed and schedule (the legacy RGB-only data path noted in the
repository is not used for this row). It is also worth naming row A1 (MobileNetV3-Small
backbone, plain decoder, cross-entropy) for what it is---a compact baseline at the same
\num{1.06}\,M parameters. Measured against it, the prior and the recall objective together add
\num{0.244} small-object recall (\num{0.366}$\rightarrow$\num{0.610}) within the compact
regime, a cleaner controlled comparison than anything against the \num{22}--\num{26}\,M
baselines. The two integration modes are within \num{0.02} mIoU of each other (\num{0.590} concat vs.\ \num{0.572} gate) and
we did not measure run-to-run variance, so we treat them as comparable and pick the gate for
its artisanal-recall edge. The prior is demonstrably used: the trained gate strengths
$\alpha_s = \{1.38, 1.42, 1.17, 1.17\}$ (coarse to fine) are all positive and near one, so
the attention map roughly doubles the modulated skip features wherever the spectral evidence
is strong. As an independent check of what the prior encodes, we use the gate's attention map
directly as a pixel-wise foreground detector: against the test-split masks it reaches an
ROC-AUC of \num{0.638} ($n=\num{3.2}$\,M pixels, a systematic 1-in-13 subsample across
\num{64} test batches)---well above chance ($0.5$) but well short of a standalone segmenter, in line with its role as a soft, spectrally-grounded prior that the
recall-weighted decoder refines rather than a segmentation output on its own.
Figure~\ref{fig:ablbars} summarizes the mining-class IoUs across variants.

\subsection{Geographic generalization}\label{sec:results-gen}
For Table~\ref{tab:gen} we withhold Asia and Oceania from training and test on them. The
gated model scores \num{0.734} mIoU on the held-out continents against \num{0.618}
in-region---but we resist reading the higher out-of-region number as positive transfer.
Holding out Asia and Oceania drops \num{51}\,\% of the chips (and \num{52}\,\% of the
artisanal chips), so the two partitions differ in size and in class and object-size mix
(Table~\ref{tab:region}). For the same reason the in-region \num{0.618} is not comparable to
the \num{0.572} of Table~\ref{tab:main}, which was trained on every region. What we can
safely say is modest: accuracy does not \emph{collapse} on two unseen continents, so this
single, composition-unmatched hold-out shows no catastrophic domain shift. A full
leave-each-region-out rotation with matched composition, and the baselines run under the same
protocol, we leave to future work.

\subsection{Efficiency and operational feasibility}\label{sec:results-eff}
Table~\ref{tab:eff} profiles inference cost. SPEAR-Net uses \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ fewer
parameters and \num{26}--\num{62}$\times$ fewer FLOPs than the baselines, but those savings
do \emph{not} show up as lower GPU latency: at \num{1.06}\,M parameters the model never fills
the accelerator, so its per-chip GPU time (\SI{7.9}{\milli\second}) is close to, and
sometimes above, the baselines. The gain lands instead exactly where deployment is
constrained. CPU latency is \num{4.7}--\num{12}$\times$ lower (\SI{43}{\milli\second} vs.\
\SI{204}{}--\SI{518}{\milli\second}), and peak GPU memory is \num{5}--\num{11}$\times$ lower
(\SI{23}{\mega\byte} vs.\ \SI{119}{}--\SI{265}{\mega\byte}). Full coverage of one
$10\,980^2$-pixel Sentinel-2 tile takes about \num{1849} non-overlapping chips ($43^2$). On
the T4 every model clears that in well under a minute, so the GPU is not where they part
company; the CPU is. Single-threaded, SPEAR-Net screens a full tile in roughly
\SI{79}{\second} (\SI{42.7}{\milli\second}/chip), against about \num{7.4}\,min for U-Net and
\num{6}--\num{16}\,min for the other baselines---an order of magnitude that decides whether
credential-free, commodity-hardware monitoring is practical.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Two findings carry the paper. The first is a non-result that matters: on the test split our
\num{1.06}\,M-parameter model matches baselines \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ its size on mIoU,
so shrinking the network costs nothing in accuracy here. The second is the useful one. The
six-band spectral prior recovers more of the hardest small sites than those much larger
baselines (Table~\ref{tab:area})---the exact regime the ASM-monitoring literature keeps
flagging as the breaking point. An RGB-only prior does not reproduce the gain, which pins it
to the NIR/SWIR bands rather than to extra capacity or the gating mechanism on its own. The
recall-oriented objective pulls its weight too: it raises recall over a plain cross-entropy
backbone and keeps both mining scales alive instead of letting the network collapse to
background.

\paragraph{On the spectral prior and its integration.}
We make no claim that the learned gate is more accurate than plain concatenation---on test
mIoU the two sit within \num{0.02}, and we did not measure run-to-run variance. If anything,
concatenation gives the best small-object recall, while the gate gives the highest artisanal
recall plus an explicit attention map built from named indices; the positive learned
strengths ($\alpha_s\approx1.2$--$1.4$) show the prior is genuinely used. So we present the
gate as the recall-oriented, interpretable choice and concatenation as the equally accurate,
slightly cheaper one. Being explicit about this points to where the work is actually
done---the spectral bands and the recall loss---instead of crediting a component the data
does not back.

\paragraph{Efficiency, read honestly.}
The compactness advantage is real but selective (Table~\ref{tab:eff}). On a GPU the model is
no faster than the baselines---\num{1.06}\,M parameters simply do not fill the
accelerator---so the win is elsewhere: \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ fewer parameters,
\num{26}--\num{62}$\times$ fewer FLOPs, \num{4.7}--\num{12}$\times$ lower CPU latency and
\num{5}--\num{11}$\times$ lower peak memory. The honest deployment claim is thus about the
edge and the CPU---fitting inside a free-tier or commodity machine and running without a
GPU---not about raw throughput.

\paragraph{Policy implications.}
At \num{0.44}--\num{0.61} small-site recall---and, just as important, an artisanal precision
near \num{0.43}, meaning about \num{57}\,\% of the pixels flagged as artisanal are false
alarms---the system is a \emph{screening and prioritisation} aid, not an enforcement-grade
detector. The recall-weighted objective buys sensitivity at the cost of precision, which is
the right trade for an inspection queue a human then triages, but it means neither a positive
nor a negative stands on its own: a flag is a lead to be checked in the field, and a missed
small site (many are below $\sim$\num{10}\,ha) is not evidence of absence. What still makes
the output policy-relevant is the verified artisanal-versus-industrial distinction, since the
two scales carry very different regulatory and environmental consequences. In deployment an
agency would set the decision threshold to its own inspection capacity---trading recall
against false-alarm load---rather than accept the single operating point we report.

\paragraph{Responsible use.}
Artisanal mining is, for many communities, a primary livelihood, and its mapping is
dual-use. We intend these methods for aggregate monitoring, environmental impact assessment
and the prioritisation of regulatory attention, not for targeting individual sites or
persons without ground verification and due process; the false-negative and false-positive
rates reported here make unsupervised targeting inappropriate. Outputs should be treated as
probabilistic leads subject to field validation and to the rights of affected communities.

\paragraph{Preprocessing caveats.}
Each site is represented by a single-date L2A acquisition with no scene-classification
cloud/shadow masking, and the \num{20}\,m SWIR bands are upsampled to \num{10}\,m; since
three of the four indices depend on SWIR, the prior inherits sensitivity to residual cloud,
phenology and atmospheric state. Robustness to these factors, and multi-temporal
compositing, are natural extensions.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Beyond the above: (i) results are single-seed---the large efficiency and small-object gaps
are unambiguous, but the sub-\num{0.02} mIoU differences among compact variants should be
read as comparable, and multi-seed variance would strengthen the claims; (ii) we did not
train an efficiency-oriented baseline, so the compute numbers are a profile, not a
comparison against other compact models; (iii) the test split is small
($\approx$\num{101} tiles actually contribute chips, of the \num{116} that carry a split) and,
like training, class-imbalanced but in the opposite direction (Table~\ref{tab:data}), which
the reader should weigh when interpreting per-class figures;
(iv) the generalization evidence is a single, composition-unmatched continent hold-out; and
(v) the edge stream is bundled with the gate in the only configuration that includes it and
the Tversky $(\alpha,\beta)$ were not swept, so neither is independently isolated.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
We presented SPEAR-Net, a compact, spectrally grounded, recall-oriented network for telling
artisanal mining apart from industrial mining in globally distributed Sentinel-2 imagery. On
a held-out test split it matches convolutional baselines \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ larger
and \num{26}--\num{62}$\times$ more costly on mIoU, recovers both mining scales without
collapsing to background, and gives the highest recall on the policy-critical artisanal
class. Its strongest positive result lands where it matters most: the six-band spectral
prior recovers more of the hardest small ($<32^2$\,px) sites than the much larger baselines,
and an RGB-only prior does not---which isolates the value of the NIR/SWIR bands. The
efficiency advantage is real but selective. It shows up in parameters, FLOPs, CPU latency
and memory, not in GPU throughput, so the deployment case is edge and free-tier screening
rather than raw speed. We report all of this alongside a frank account of what remains open:
at \num{0.44}--\num{0.61} small-site recall the tool prioritises inspection rather than
proving absence, and single-seed training, a small industrial-heavy test split, and a single
composition-unmatched continent hold-out bound what we can claim. The natural next steps are
the small-site regime itself (multi-scale and multi-temporal inputs), multi-seed runs and a
full leave-each-region-out rotation with matched composition, and transfer to other ASM
settings. For that transfer we have earmarked complementary public
resources: Brazilian \emph{garimpo} (artisanal) masks\footnote{\url{https://gitlab.com/luizcf14/brazil-mining}}
to add small-scale supervision and an Amazon out-of-domain test, a UAV illegal-mining
collection\footnote{\url{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/lviacecliagomessilva/illegal-mining-dataset}}
for a cross-platform stress test, and the global mining-footprint polygons of Tang and
Werner\footnote{Zenodo record~6806817, \url{https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.6806817}.}
\cite{tang2023mining} as a source of additional positives and hard negatives.
