\begin{abstract}
Artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM), frequently unregulated or illegal, is spatially
fragmented and spectrally similar to bare soil, rock and turbid water, so it is routinely
missed by segmentation models and confused with background; meanwhile, continental
monitoring on free-tier hardware calls for compact, low-latency networks. We present
\emph{SPEAR-Net}, a compact (\num{1.06}\,M-parameter, \num{0.60}\,GFLOPs at $256^2$)
segmentation network for mapping mining on Sentinel-2 imagery. It computes four spectral
indices---NDVI, MNDWI, NDTI and BSI---on the fly from the six visible-to-shortwave-infrared
bands and fuses them as an explainable attention prior over the decoder, and is trained with
a recall-weighted, boundary-aware objective for small, fragmented targets. We evaluate on a
globally distributed, expert-verified dataset with an explicit
\emph{artisanal-versus-industrial} label. On the held-out test split
of the three-class task, SPEAR-Net reaches \num{0.57}--\num{0.62} mIoU, on par with
U-Net, DeepLabV3+ and U-Net++ (\num{0.56}--\num{0.60}) despite \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$
fewer parameters and \num{26}--\num{62}$\times$ fewer floating-point operations. More
importantly, an object-size analysis shows that the six-band spectral prior \emph{improves
recovery of the hardest small sites}: component recall on small ($<32^2$\,px) objects
reaches \num{0.61} (concatenated prior) and \num{0.54} (gated prior), exceeding every
baseline (\num{0.42}--\num{0.51}) and an RGB-only prior (\num{0.44}), so the gain is
specifically attributable to the near-infrared/shortwave-infrared bands. The gated variant
attains the highest recall on the policy-critical artisanal class (\num{0.75}). Efficiency
gains concentrate where deployment is constrained---\num{5}--\num{12}$\times$ lower CPU
latency and \num{5}--\num{11}$\times$ lower peak memory than the baselines---rather than in
GPU throughput. We position SPEAR-Net as an accurate, frugal screening tool for large-area
artisanal-mining monitoring, and we report an honest diagnosis of the remaining small-site
error and the composition effects that bound the conclusions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Mining reshapes land surfaces at a global scale, and its most policy-sensitive form
---artisanal and small-scale mining (ASM)---is often informal or illegal, environmentally
damaging, and difficult to govern precisely because it is small, transient and spatially
scattered \citep{maus2022global,gallwey2020asm}. Earth-observation satellites such as
Sentinel-2 \citep{drusch2012sentinel2} provide the frequent, free, multispectral coverage
needed to monitor mining over large areas, and semantic segmentation of satellite imagery
is the natural tool for delineating mining footprints. Yet a recurring failure mode is
reported across sensors and architectures: small, fragmented, low-density mining is
under-detected and confused with bare soil, rock and water, producing low recall and false
alarms \citep{gallwey2020asm,balaniuk2020mining}. This failure disproportionately affects
ASM, which is the very class of greatest regulatory interest.

Two practical constraints compound the scientific difficulty. First, the strongest public
mining annotations are heavy: pixel-wise datasets can reach tens of gigabytes and hundreds
of thousands of tiles, which is impractical on the free-tier accelerators (e.g. single
consumer or cloud GPUs with modest memory) available to many operational and academic
users. Second, prior work rarely reports the computational cost of the proposed model,
even though deployability---parameter count, floating-point operations (FLOPs) and
latency---is decisive for continental-scale or edge inference.

We argue that these constraints can be turned into design principles. We build on a
globally distributed, expert-verified mining dataset whose annotation package is compact
(tens of megabytes) and whose imagery is fetched on demand from an open archive, so a
representative subset trains comfortably on free-tier hardware. Crucially, this dataset
carries an explicit, manually validated \emph{scale} attribute (artisanal vs.\ industrial),
making the small-scale/illegal-prone class \emph{trainable} rather than a near-empty pixel
category. Fetching the raw bands ourselves restores the near-infrared (NIR) and
shortwave-infrared (SWIR) channels, enabling a spectrally grounded prior, and the
global footprint enables a geographic generalization protocol seldom evaluated in the
mining literature.

Concretely, we make the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Artisanal-versus-industrial mapping on verified labels with a compact model.}
We frame mining segmentation around the policy-relevant scale distinction and show that a
\num{1.06}\,M-parameter, \num{0.60}\,GFLOPs network is on par on test mIoU with
convolutional baselines that are \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ larger and \num{26}--\num{62}$\times$
more costly (Section~\ref{sec:results-main}).
\item \textbf{A spectral prior that helps precisely where scale does not.} Our
spectral-index prior improves recall on the hardest small ($<32^2$\,px) sites beyond
what \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ larger baselines achieve, and an RGB-only variant does not,
isolating the contribution of the NIR/SWIR bands (Sections~\ref{sec:results-area},
\ref{sec:results-ablation}).
\item \textbf{A recall-oriented objective for fragmented targets.} A compound
present-class-Dice, Tversky, focal and boundary/edge loss recovers both mining scales
without collapsing to background and gives the gated model the highest recall on the
policy-critical artisanal class.
\item \textbf{An honest, reproducible evaluation}: an area-stratified diagnosis of where
detection fails, a compute profile that separates the metrics on which a small model does
and does not win (CPU latency and memory vs.\ GPU throughput), and a one-click, free-tier,
credential-free pipeline; all code and configurations are released.
\end{enumerate}

We report single-run results and deliberately frame our claims as \emph{parity plus
efficiency} rather than accuracy superiority; where component differences are within the
margin one would expect from run-to-run variation, we say so explicitly. All code, configs
and the data-preparation pipeline are released for reproducibility.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Mining and ASM mapping from satellite imagery.}
Global inventories of mining land use have been assembled from manual and semi-automatic
interpretation of optical imagery \citep{maus2022global} and, more recently, from
high-resolution satellite mapping \citep{tang2023mining}. Deep learning is increasingly
applied to mining footprints: \citet{saputra2025multimodal} performed multi-modal semantic
segmentation of mining land covers across 37 sites worldwide, and
\citet{balaniuk2020mining} addressed mining and tailings-dam detection at national scale.
Multi-class monitoring systems such as MineCam combine segmentation and change detection
over annotated mine components \citep{jablonska2024minecam}. For ASM specifically,
\citet{gallwey2020asm} trained a multispectral convolutional network to detect small-scale
mining in Ghana, \citet{ngom2020senegal} mapped artisanal gold mining in Senegal from
Sentinel-2 data, \citet{couttenier2022asm} mapped ASM over 1.75 million~km$^2$ of West
Africa with a segmentation network, and \citet{fonseca2024asm} improved ASM detection with
spectral--textural segmentation of Landsat time series. A recent systematic review of ASM
mapping \citep{nursamsi2024review} concludes that approaches which succeed locally do not
yet scale to regional or global monitoring. A consistent theme across these studies is that
fragmented, low-density mining is hard to recover and easily confused with spectrally
similar background, motivating methods that inject physical priors and explicitly optimize
recall; moreover, none of them reports the computational cost of the deployed model, which
is central here.

\paragraph{Semantic segmentation architectures.}
Since fully convolutional networks established dense prediction \citep{long2015fcn},
encoder--decoder networks with skip connections, epitomized by U-Net
\citep{ronneberger2015unet} and its successors U-Net++ \citep{zhou2019unetpp} and
Attention U-Net \citep{oktay2018attentionunet}, have remained strong baselines, as have
context-aggregation designs such as PSPNet \citep{zhao2017pspnet}, atrous
encoder--decoders such as DeepLabV3+ \citep{chen2018deeplabv3plus} and transformer
segmenters such as SegFormer \citep{xie2021segformer}. These models are accurate but
parameter-heavy (tens of millions of weights). Efficiency-oriented backbones---MobileNetV2/V3
\citep{sandler2018mobilenetv2,howard2019mobilenetv3}, EfficientNet
\citep{tan2019efficientnet} and depthwise-separable convolutions
\citep{chollet2017xception}---enable compact segmenters, but their accuracy--efficiency
trade-off for fine-grained mining segmentation has not been systematically reported.

\paragraph{Spectral indices and prior-guided attention.}
Normalized-difference indices such as NDVI \citep{rouse1974ndvi} and MNDWI
\citep{xu2006mndwi}, and soil/turbidity indices such as BSI \citep{rikimaru2002bsi}, encode
domain knowledge about vegetation, water and bare ground. In mining remote sensing they are
typically used as hand-crafted features for classical classifiers, or fed as raw extra
channels, rather than as a \emph{learned}, spatially explicit attention prior inside a
modern segmentation network. We revisit these indices as a differentiable, explainable gate.

\paragraph{Imbalanced-segmentation losses.}
Region-overlap losses (Dice \citep{milletari2016vnet,sudre2017generaliseddice}, Tversky
\citep{salehi2017tversky}), the focal loss \citep{lin2020focal} and boundary-aware losses
\citep{kervadec2021boundary} were introduced to counter class imbalance and to sharpen
object boundaries. We combine them into a single recall-oriented objective tailored to the
small, fragmented artisanal class.

\paragraph{Geospatial foundation models.}
A parallel line of work pre-trains large transformer backbones on massive unlabelled
satellite archives---masked-autoencoder models such as SatMAE \citep{cong2022satmae},
geospatial foundation models (e.g.\ Prithvi, Clay), and the promptable Segment Anything Model
\citep{kirillov2023sam} adapted to Earth observation---and fine-tunes them for downstream
tasks. These models can be
powerful when abundant compute and labels are available, but they carry hundreds of millions
of parameters and are ill-suited to the free-tier and CPU-only budgets that constrain much
operational and academic ASM monitoring. Our aim is complementary: a compact, transparent
model whose inductive bias comes from explicit spectral indices rather than from large-scale
pre-training, so that it trains and runs within commodity resources while remaining
interpretable. Distilling or adapting a foundation model under the same budget is an
orthogonal and promising direction.

\section{Materials and methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Dataset}\label{sec:data}
We use a globally distributed, machine-learning-ready mining dataset
\citep{jasansky2024dataset} that reconciles and manually re-validates polygons derived from
\citet{maus2022global} and \citet{tang2023mining}. The annotation package is compact and
provides, per site, the exact Sentinel-2 scene reference, mask polygons, a train/validation/
test split, and two metadata attributes: a mine-type label (surface, placer, underground,
brine/evaporation) and a \emph{scale} label---\emph{artisanal} versus \emph{industrial}---
which is the attribute of primary interest here. The annotation layer contains
\num{1514} tiles drawn from \num{1128} distinct Sentinel-2 scenes; of these, \num{1207}
tiles carry a train/validation/test split (\num{890}/\num{201}/\num{116}) and are the ones
we use. Over all tiles the scale label is \num{329} artisanal and \num{1185} industrial,
and over the split subset it is \num{232}/\num{975} (Table~\ref{tab:data}). Reported
annotation quality on the test set is high (precision \textgreater\,\num{0.99}, recall
\textgreater\,\num{0.95}), which makes the scale attribute reliable enough to serve as a
training target. The geographic distribution of the sites is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:map};
both scale classes span South America, Africa, Asia and Oceania, which is what later
enables the held-out continent protocol of Section~\ref{sec:results-gen}.

\subsection{Imagery retrieval and chipping}\label{sec:prep}
Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface-reflectance imagery is retrieved on demand from an open cloud
archive \citep{planetarycomputer}. For each tile we resolve the corresponding scene by its
grid tile and acquisition date and load six bands---blue (B2), green (B3), red (B4),
near-infrared (B8), and shortwave-infrared SWIR1 (B11) and SWIR2 (B12)---resampled to a
common \SI{10}{\meter} grid over a $2048\times2048$ window centred on the site. Mask
polygons are rasterized onto the same grid and coordinate reference system. Windows are cut
into non-overlapping $256\times256$ chips. Mining-containing chips are all kept; mining-free
chips are retained with probability \num{0.3} as negatives (yielding \num{9034} positive and
\num{6237} negative chips). To keep the pipeline light we retrieve \emph{all} artisanal
tiles and cap industrial tiles at \num{80} per split, giving \num{15271} chips; during
training, artisanal chips are additionally oversampled by a factor of three. The
\num{20}\,m SWIR bands (B11, B12) are bilinearly resampled to the \num{10}\,m grid so that
all six bands share one raster, which the reader should note because three of the four
indices depend on SWIR. Reflectance digital numbers are scaled by \num{10000} to
approximate surface reflectance $\rho\in[0,1]$. Beyond the L2A product's own atmospheric
correction we apply \emph{no} scene-classification cloud/shadow masking; each site is
represented by a single acquisition. Chips inherit their tile's split, so no site
contributes chips to more than one split (no spatial leakage). No further preprocessing is
applied; the spectral indices below are computed on the fly inside the data loader.

\subsection{Overview of SPEAR-Net}\label{sec:arch}
SPEAR-Net is an encoder--decoder segmenter with three additions targeted at fine-grained
mining: (i) an ImageNet-pretrained lightweight encoder whose stem is adapted from three to
six input bands; (ii) a spectral-index prior (PISP) that modulates the
decoder skip connections; and (iii) a segmentation head paired with an auxiliary
edge/boundary head for deep supervision. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the architecture and
Algorithm~\ref{alg:spearnet} summarizes a full training step.

\paragraph{Encoder.}
We use MobileNetV3-Small \citep{howard2019mobilenetv3} pretrained on ImageNet
\citep{deng2009imagenet} as a feature extractor, returning multi-scale features at strides
$2$ to $32$. To accept six bands, the first convolution is expanded from three to six input
channels, initializing the additional NIR/SWIR filters by tiling and rescaling the
pretrained RGB filters so that pretrained low-level features are preserved. The decoder is a
U-Net-style path built from depthwise-separable convolution blocks
\citep{chollet2017xception} with channel widths $\{128,64,48,32\}$, progressively upsampling
and fusing gated skip features.

\subsection{Spectral-index prior (PISP)}\label{sec:pisp}
We term this a \emph{spectral-index prior} (PISP) because its channels are normalized band ratios
grounded in the physical reflectance behaviour of surface materials---chlorophyll
absorption and near-infrared scattering for vegetation, strong SWIR absorption by water,
and the high visible/SWIR reflectance of exposed soil---rather than being learned from data;
the same quantities are standard physical proxies in the remote-sensing literature
\citep{rouse1974ndvi,xu2006mndwi,rikimaru2002bsi}. From the reflectance bands
$\rho_{\mathrm{B}},\rho_{\mathrm{G}},\rho_{\mathrm{R}},\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}},
\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}},\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR2}}$ we compute four indices that encode the
dominant visual cues of mining---loss of vegetation, presence of ponds and turbid water,
and exposed bare ground/tailings:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{NDVI}  &= \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}-\rho_{\mathrm{R}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}+\rho_{\mathrm{R}}}, \label{eq:ndvi}\\[2pt]
\mathrm{MNDWI} &= \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{G}}-\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{G}}+\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}}}, \label{eq:mndwi}\\[2pt]
\mathrm{NDTI}  &= \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{R}}-\rho_{\mathrm{G}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{R}}+\rho_{\mathrm{G}}}, \label{eq:ndti}\\[2pt]
\mathrm{BSI}   &= \frac{(\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}}+\rho_{\mathrm{R}})-(\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}+\rho_{\mathrm{B}})}
                       {(\rho_{\mathrm{SWIR1}}+\rho_{\mathrm{R}})+(\rho_{\mathrm{NIR}}+\rho_{\mathrm{B}})}. \label{eq:bsi}
\end{align}
Stacking the four indices gives a tensor
$\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times H\times W}$ (NDVI is vegetation
\citep{rouse1974ndvi}, MNDWI water \citep{xu2006mndwi}, NDTI turbidity
\citep{lacaux2007ndti}, BSI bare soil \citep{rikimaru2002bsi}). A small convolutional gate maps $\mathbf{P}$ to a single-channel
spatial attention map,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{A} = \sigma\!\big(\, g(\mathbf{P}) \,\big),\qquad
g(\mathbf{P}) = \mathrm{Conv}_{1\times1}\!\big(\phi\big(\mathrm{DWConv}_{3\times3}(\phi(\mathrm{Conv}_{1\times1}(\mathbf{P})))\big)\big),
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is a batch-normalized ReLU, $\mathrm{DWConv}$ a depthwise convolution and
$\sigma$ the logistic function, so $\mathbf{A}\in(0,1)^{1\times H\times W}$. The map is
applied multiplicatively to each decoder skip feature $\mathbf{F}_s$ at scale $s$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{F}'_s = \mathbf{F}_s \odot \big(1 + \alpha_s\, \mathbf{A}_{\downarrow s}\big),
\label{eq:apply}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}_{\downarrow s}$ is $\mathbf{A}$ bilinearly resized to the resolution of
scale $s$, $\alpha_s$ is a learnable per-scale scalar, and $\odot$ is broadcast elementwise
multiplication. The residual form $1+\alpha_s\mathbf{A}$ lets the network fall back to the
ungated feature when the prior is uninformative. Because $\mathbf{A}$ is a single explicit
map derived from named indices, it is directly interpretable as ``where the spectral
evidence for mining is strongest''. As an ablation we also consider a simpler
\emph{concatenation} variant, in which $\mathbf{P}$ is appended to the input bands and
linearly projected back to six channels, and an \emph{RGB-only} variant in which the same
gate is driven by three colour indices computed from the visible bands alone.

\subsection{Recall-weighted, boundary-aware objective}\label{sec:loss}
Let $p_{c}$ and $g_{c}$ denote the predicted probability and one-hot ground truth for class
$c$, and let $\mathcal{C}^{+}$ be the set of classes present in a batch. We combine four
terms. A generalized Dice term \citep{sudre2017generaliseddice},
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}} = 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}^{+}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}^{+}}
\frac{2\sum_i p_{c,i} g_{c,i} + \epsilon}{\sum_i p_{c,i} + \sum_i g_{c,i} + \epsilon},
\label{eq:dice}
\end{equation}
averaged \emph{only over present classes} to avoid a spurious penalty that would otherwise
drive the network toward the majority (background) class. A Tversky term
\citep{salehi2017tversky} with $\alpha<\beta$ penalizes false negatives more than false
positives, directly favouring recall of the rare classes:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tv}} = 1 - \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}^{+}|}\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}^{+}}
\frac{\sum_i p_{c,i} g_{c,i}}
     {\sum_i p_{c,i} g_{c,i} + \alpha\sum_i p_{c,i}(1-g_{c,i}) + \beta\sum_i (1-p_{c,i}) g_{c,i}}.
\label{eq:tversky}
\end{equation}
A class-weighted focal term \citep{lin2020focal} supplies a per-pixel gradient that focuses
on hard, rare-class pixels,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} w_{y_i}\, (1-p_{y_i,i})^{\gamma}\, \log p_{y_i,i},
\label{eq:focal}
\end{equation}
with $w$ the inverse-frequency class weights and $\gamma$ the focusing parameter. Finally, a
boundary term concentrates a cross-entropy penalty on the ground-truth object boundaries
$\mathbf{B}$ (the Sobel edges of the target mask), and an auxiliary head predicts those
boundaries directly:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}} = \frac{\sum_i \mathbf{B}_i\, \mathrm{CE}_i}{\sum_i \mathbf{B}_i + \epsilon},
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{E}} = \mathrm{BCE}\big(\sigma(\hat{\mathbf{e}}),\, \mathbf{B}\big).
\label{eq:boundary}
\end{equation}
The total objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\mathrm{D}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}}
            + \lambda_{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tv}}
            + \lambda_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}}
            + \lambda_{\mathrm{B}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}
            + \lambda_{\mathrm{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{E}}.
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{SPEAR-Net training step for one mini-batch}
\label{alg:spearnet}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require six-band patch $\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{6\times H\times W}$, ground-truth mask
$\mathbf{Y}$, parameters $\theta$, per-scale gate weights $\{\alpha_k\}$
\Statex \textbf{Physically-informed spectral prior}
\State $\mathbf{P}\gets[\mathrm{NDVI},\mathrm{MNDWI},\mathrm{NDTI},\mathrm{BSI}]$ from $\mathbf{X}$ \Comment{Eqs.~\eqref{eq:ndvi}--\eqref{eq:bsi}}
\State $\mathbf{A}\gets\sigma\!\big(g(\mathbf{P})\big)$ \Comment{single-channel attention map, Eq.~\eqref{eq:gate}}
\Statex \textbf{Encoder (MobileNetV3-Small, strides $2$--$32$)}
\State $(\mathbf{s}_1,\dots,\mathbf{s}_5)\gets\mathrm{Encoder}(\mathbf{X})$
\Statex \textbf{Prior-gated depthwise-separable decoder}
\State $\mathbf{u}\gets\mathbf{s}_5$ \Comment{ungated bottleneck}
\For{$k=4$ \textbf{down to} $1$}
  \State $\mathbf{u}\gets\mathrm{Upsample}(\mathbf{u})$ to the resolution of $\mathbf{s}_k$
  \State $\mathbf{F}'_k\gets\mathbf{s}_k\odot\big(1+\alpha_k\,\mathbf{A}_{\downarrow k}\big)$ \Comment{gate skip, Eq.~\eqref{eq:apply}}
  \State $\mathbf{u}\gets\mathrm{DSConv}\big([\,\mathbf{u};\,\mathbf{F}'_k\,]\big)$ \Comment{concatenate, then refine}
\EndFor
\State $\mathbf{u}\gets\mathrm{Upsample}_{256^2}(\mathbf{u})$
\Statex \textbf{Heads, objective, and update}
\State $\hat{\mathbf{Y}}\gets\mathrm{SegHead}(\mathbf{u})$;\quad $\hat{\mathbf{e}}\gets\mathrm{EdgeHead}(\mathbf{u})$
\State $\mathbf{B}\gets\mathrm{Sobel}(\mathbf{Y})$ \Comment{boundary target}
\State $\mathcal{L}\gets\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Dice}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{T}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Tv}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{F}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{B}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{B}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{E}}$ \Comment{Eqs.~\eqref{eq:dice}--\eqref{eq:total}}
\State $\theta\gets\mathrm{AdamW}\big(\theta,\ \nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}\big)$
\State \Return updated $\theta$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Training details}\label{sec:training}
Unless stated otherwise we use
$\lambda_{\mathrm{D}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{T}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{F}}=1$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{B}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{E}}=0.5$, Tversky $(\alpha,\beta)=(0.3,0.7)$, and
focal $\gamma=2$. Class weights $w$ are estimated by median-frequency balancing on a random
sample of masks, and chips containing the rare (artisanal) class are oversampled. Models are
trained with AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} at a base learning rate of
$3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, a cosine schedule \citep{loshchilov2017sgdr} with a
three-epoch linear warm-up, mixed-precision, gradient clipping at norm $1$, batch size
$10$, and $40$ epochs. Implementation uses PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch}; convolutional
baselines use the implementations of \citet{iakubovskii2019smp} with ResNet-34 encoders
\citep{he2016resnet} and the same six-band input, loss and schedule for a controlled
comparison. Training used a single NVIDIA A100
GPU; the model also fits and trains within the memory of a free-tier 16\,GB accelerator.

\subsection{Evaluation}\label{sec:eval}
With $\mathrm{TP}_c$, $\mathrm{FP}_c$ and $\mathrm{FN}_c$ the pixel-level true positives,
false positives and false negatives of class $c$ accumulated over the evaluation set, we
report the per-class Intersection-over-Union and recall,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IoU}_c \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{TP}_c}{\mathrm{TP}_c+\mathrm{FP}_c+\mathrm{FN}_c},
\qquad
R_c \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{TP}_c}{\mathrm{TP}_c+\mathrm{FN}_c},
\label{eq:iou}
\end{equation}
and their means over the $C$ classes present in the ground truth,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{mIoU} \;=\; \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}\mathrm{IoU}_c,
\qquad
\overline{R} \;=\; \frac{1}{C}\sum_{c=1}^{C} R_c .
\label{eq:miou}
\end{equation}
To probe the field-wide small-object failure directly, we additionally compute an
\emph{area-stratified recall} at the object level. Let
$\mathcal{K}_b=\{K_1,\dots,K_{n_b}\}$ be the connected components of the foreground ground
truth whose pixel area falls in bin $b\in\{$small $<32^2$, medium, large $\geq96^2\}$; a
component counts as recovered if at least one of its pixels is predicted with the correct
class, giving
\begin{equation}
R^{\mathrm{area}}_b \;=\; \frac{1}{n_b}\,
\bigl|\{\,K\in\mathcal{K}_b : \hat{Y}\cap K \neq \varnothing \,\}\bigr| ,
\label{eq:areastrat}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{Y}$ denotes the set of pixels predicted as the component's class. This metric
is deliberately lenient at the pixel level so that it isolates \emph{detection} of a site
from delineation quality. Efficiency is reported as parameter count and
multiply--accumulate operations (GFLOPs) at $256^2$ input. For geographic generalization
we assign each chip to a continent from its centroid and perform a held-out continent
experiment, training with two continents held out and testing on them.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
All numbers below are on the held-out \emph{test} split; the validation split was used only
for checkpoint selection. All models share the six-band input, the compound loss and the
training schedule, and were trained for \num{40} epochs (single run).

\subsection{Accuracy and the artisanal--industrial distinction}\label{sec:results-main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports test-split accuracy. On mIoU the compact SPEAR-Net variants
(\num{0.572}--\num{0.590}) and the RGB variant (\num{0.620}) are interleaved with the
\num{21}--\num{26}\,M-parameter baselines (\num{0.562}--\num{0.604}); no consistent accuracy
gap separates the two groups, so the order-of-magnitude reduction in size does not cost
accuracy on this split. Foreground mIoU (background excluded) tells the same story
(\num{0.41}--\num{0.47} across all models). On the policy-critical artisanal class, the
gated model attains the highest recall of any model (\num{0.748} vs.\ \num{0.647}--\num{0.662}
for the baselines), consistent with its recall-oriented design. The baselines retain a small
edge on the industrial class, which dominates the industrial-heavy test split
(Table~\ref{tab:data}). Figure~\ref{fig:efficiency} plots accuracy against parameter count.

The pixel-level confusion matrix of the gated model (Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}) makes the
error structure explicit. Recall on the diagonal is \num{0.926}/\num{0.748}/\num{0.662} for
background/artisanal/industrial. The dominant artisanal error is omission: \num{24.2}\,\% of
artisanal pixels are assigned to background, whereas only \num{1.0}\,\% are confused with
industrial. The scale confusion is therefore small---an artisanal site is rarely mislabelled
as regulated industrial mining---but the background-omission rate is the operational
bottleneck and is consistent with the small-site recall deficit analysed in
Section~\ref{sec:results-area}. From an enforcement standpoint both artisanal off-diagonals
are false negatives (a missed illegal-prone site), which is why we report recall, not
accuracy, as the headline metric.

\subsection{Where detection fails, and what fixes it}\label{sec:results-area}
The operationally decisive question is not overall mIoU but whether small, fragmented sites
are detected at all. Table~\ref{tab:area} reports object-level recall stratified by
ground-truth component size (Eq.~\ref{eq:areastrat}) for every model. Two results stand out.
First, recall rises steeply with size for all models---from roughly \num{0.4} on small
($<32^2$\,px) objects to \num{0.8}--\num{0.9} on large ones---so the small-site regime is the
common bottleneck, and it is \emph{not} closed by the \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ larger
baselines (small-object recall \num{0.42}--\num{0.51}). Second, the six-band spectral prior
\emph{does} help there: the concatenated prior reaches \num{0.610} and the gated prior
\num{0.537} on small objects, both above every baseline, whereas the RGB-only prior
(\num{0.439}) does not, isolating the effect to the NIR/SWIR bands. In other words, on the
hardest and most policy-relevant regime a \num{1.06}\,M-parameter spectrally-informed model
recovers more small sites than convolutional networks with \num{20}--\num{25}$\times$ more
parameters. We nonetheless read the absolute value plainly: at \num{0.44}--\num{0.61}
small-object recall the tool supports \emph{screening and prioritisation}, not
enforcement-grade detection.

\subsection{Component analysis (ablation)}\label{sec:results-ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} isolates each component. All rows use identical chips, splits,
random seed and schedule; the RGB variant uses the same six-band chips restricted to the
visible bands, so the six-band-vs-RGB comparison is controlled. Adding the recall-weighted
objective to the plain backbone lifts overall recall (\num{0.573}/\num{0.586} $\rightarrow$
\num{0.605}/\num{0.627} on the two mining classes) and small-object recall
(\num{0.366}$\rightarrow$\num{0.463}). Adding the six-band spectral prior improves small-object
recall further, most sharply in the concatenated form (\num{0.610}); the gated form trades a
little of that for the highest artisanal recall (\num{0.748}). The RGB variant, in contrast,
\emph{loses} small-object recall (\num{0.439}), confirming that the benefit comes from the
NIR/SWIR bands rather than the gating mechanism per se. The two integration modes are close
on mIoU (\num{0.590} concat vs.\ \num{0.572} gate) and we did not measure run-to-run
variance, so we treat them as comparable and select the gate for its artisanal-recall
advantage. That the prior is actually used is evident from the learned per-scale gate
strengths of the trained model, $\alpha_s = \{1.38, 1.42, 1.17, 1.17\}$ (from coarse to
fine): all are positive and near unity, so the attention map roughly doubles the modulated
skip features where the spectral evidence is strong. As an independent check of what the
prior encodes, we use the gate's attention map directly as a pixel-wise foreground detector:
against the ground-truth mask on the test split it attains an ROC-AUC of \num{0.638}
($n=\num{3.2}$\,M pixels)---well above chance ($0.5$) but far from a standalone segmenter,
consistent with its intended role as a soft, spectrally-grounded prior that the
recall-weighted decoder refines rather than a segmentation output in itself.
Figure~\ref{fig:ablbars} summarizes the mining-class IoUs across variants.

\subsection{Efficiency and operational feasibility}\label{sec:results-eff}
Table~\ref{tab:eff} profiles inference cost. SPEAR-Net uses \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ fewer
parameters and \num{26}--\num{62}$\times$ fewer FLOPs than the baselines. These savings do
\emph{not} translate into lower GPU latency---at \num{1.06}\,M parameters the model does not
saturate the accelerator, so its per-chip GPU time (\SI{7.9}{\milli\second}) is close to,
and sometimes above, the baselines. The advantage instead appears exactly where deployment
is constrained: CPU latency is \num{5}--\num{12}$\times$ lower (\SI{43}{\milli\second} vs.\
\SI{204}{}--\SI{518}{\milli\second}) and peak GPU memory is \num{5}--\num{11}$\times$ lower
(\SI{23}{\mega\byte} vs.\ \SI{119}{}--\SI{265}{\mega\byte}). At the measured throughput one
$10\,980^2$-pixel Sentinel-2 tile ($\approx$\num{1764} chips) is screened in well under a
minute on a single GPU by any of the models, so the practical differentiator for
large-area, credential-free monitoring is the CPU/memory footprint rather than raw speed.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Two findings anchor the paper. First, on the test split a \num{1.06}\,M-parameter model is
on par on mIoU with baselines that are \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ larger, so the compactness
is not bought with accuracy. Second, and more useful operationally, the six-band spectral
prior improves recovery of the hardest small sites beyond those larger baselines
(Table~\ref{tab:area})---the regime that the ASM-monitoring literature repeatedly flags as
the failure point. Because an RGB-only prior does not produce this gain, the effect is
attributable to the NIR/SWIR bands rather than to added capacity or to the gating mechanism
alone. The recall-oriented objective is likewise effective: it lifts recall over a plain
cross-entropy backbone and recovers both mining scales rather than collapsing to background.

\paragraph{On the spectral prior and its integration.}
Between the two integration modes we do \emph{not} claim the learned gate improves accuracy
over concatenation---on test mIoU they differ by less than \num{0.02} and we did not measure
run-to-run variance. The concatenated prior in fact gives the best small-object recall,
while the gate gives the highest artisanal recall and an explicit, named-index-derived
attention map; the positive learned gate strengths ($\alpha_s\approx1.2$--$1.4$) confirm the
prior is used. We therefore present the gate as a recall-oriented and interpretable design
choice and report concatenation as the equally accurate, marginally cheaper alternative.
This candour identifies which mechanism does the work---the spectral bands and the recall
objective---rather than attributing gains to a component the data does not support.

\paragraph{Efficiency, read honestly.}
The compactness advantage is real but selective (Table~\ref{tab:eff}). On a GPU the model is
\emph{not} faster than the baselines, because \num{1.06}\,M parameters do not saturate the
accelerator; its benefits are \num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ fewer parameters,
\num{26}--\num{62}$\times$ fewer FLOPs, \num{5}--\num{12}$\times$ lower CPU latency and
\num{5}--\num{11}$\times$ lower peak memory. The relevant deployment claim is therefore edge-
and CPU-oriented---running within a free-tier or commodity machine's memory and without a
GPU---not raw throughput.

\paragraph{Policy implications.}
At \num{0.44}--\num{0.61} small-site recall the system is a \emph{screening and
prioritisation} aid, not an enforcement-grade detector: it can direct scarce inspection
capacity toward likely artisanal sites and support aggregate impact monitoring, but a
missed-site rate of this magnitude means individual negatives cannot be treated as evidence
of absence. The verified artisanal-vs-industrial distinction is what makes the output
policy-relevant, since the two scales carry very different regulatory and environmental
implications.

\paragraph{Responsible use.}
Artisanal mining is, for many communities, a primary livelihood, and its mapping is
dual-use. We intend these methods for aggregate monitoring, environmental impact assessment
and the prioritisation of regulatory attention, not for targeting individual sites or
persons without ground verification and due process; the false-negative and false-positive
rates reported here make unsupervised targeting inappropriate. Outputs should be treated as
probabilistic leads subject to field validation and to the rights of affected communities.

\paragraph{Preprocessing caveats.}
Each site is represented by a single-date L2A acquisition with no scene-classification
cloud/shadow masking, and the \num{20}\,m SWIR bands are upsampled to \num{10}\,m; since
three of the four indices depend on SWIR, the prior inherits sensitivity to residual cloud,
phenology and atmospheric state. Robustness to these factors, and multi-temporal
compositing, are natural extensions.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Beyond the above: (i) results are single-seed---the large efficiency and small-object gaps
are unambiguous, but the sub-\num{0.02} mIoU differences among compact variants should be
read as comparable, and multi-seed variance would strengthen the claims; (ii) we did not
train an efficiency-oriented baseline, so the compute numbers are a profile, not a
comparison against other compact models; (iii) the test split is small
($n=\num{116}$ tiles) and, like training, class-imbalanced but in the opposite direction
(Table~\ref{tab:data}), which the reader should weigh when interpreting per-class figures;
(iv) the generalization evidence is a single, composition-unmatched continent hold-out; and
(v) the edge stream is bundled with the gate in the only configuration that includes it and
the Tversky $(\alpha,\beta)$ were not swept, so neither is independently isolated.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
We introduced SPEAR-Net, a compact, spectrally grounded, recall-oriented network for
distinguishing artisanal from industrial mining in globally distributed Sentinel-2 imagery.
On a held-out test split it is on par on mIoU with convolutional baselines that are
\num{21}--\num{25}$\times$ larger and \num{26}--\num{62}$\times$ more costly, recovers both
mining scales without background collapse, and gives the highest recall on the
policy-critical artisanal class. Its main positive result is where it matters most: the
six-band spectral prior improves recovery of the hardest small ($<32^2$\,px) sites beyond
the much larger baselines, and an RGB-only prior does not, isolating the value of the
NIR/SWIR bands. The efficiency advantage is real but selective---parameters, FLOPs, CPU
latency and memory, not GPU throughput---so the deployment case is edge/free-tier screening.
We pair these results with an honest account of what remains: small-site recall of
\num{0.44}--\num{0.61} makes the tool a prioritisation aid rather than an enforcement-grade
detector, and single-seed training, a small industrial-heavy test split, and a single
composition-unmatched continent hold-out bound the conclusions. Future work will target the
small-site regime (multi-scale and multi-temporal inputs), multi-seed and
leave-each-region-out evaluation with matched composition, and transfer to auxiliary ASM
domains.
